How to use this document

This file does three jobs at once.

  1. Lecturer script. Each slide has a Purpose and Speaker Notes. Notes are written to be spoken, not read word for word.
  2. Build brief. Main Content is the only text that appears on screen. Visual Direction says what to show.
  3. Student guidebook. The Main Content blocks, the framework card (The Someone-Else Check), the Resources and the References are written so students can revisit them after class.

Pacing is left to the lecturer. There are more slides and interactions here than one session needs, so you can pick rounds based on the room.

The one habit this lecture should leave behind:

Stop asking only "Does this make sense to me?" Start asking "How will someone else understand this?"

Sources are numbered footnotes. The full list is in the References section at the end.

Interaction toolkit

Every interaction uses one of six formats. Each has its own tag and icon in the presentation, so students learn to recognise them quickly (this is itself recognition over recall).

Tag What it is Why it teaches the point
VOTE Quick hands-up between two or three options Shows the room disagrees, which proves "obvious" is not universal
PREDICT Guess what real users did, then reveal the data Exposes the gap between what we assume and what people do
TRICK QUESTION The obvious answer is wrong Makes students feel their own bias instead of hearing about it
COMPARE Two versions side by side; pick one and say why Trains the habit of naming the principle behind a preference
BURNING QUESTION An open debate with no single right answer Pushes students to argue from the user's side, not their own
YOUR TURN Students bring their own good or bad examples Moves from our examples to their daily apps

Answers for trick questions and predictions are marked Reveal: so the build can hide them behind a tap.

Build brief (global rules)

The presentation must practise what it teaches. The closing reflection makes this explicit.

Look and feel

  • Dark, high-contrast interface with two signature colours that carry meaning all the way through: electric blue = what the designer knows, amber = what the user understands. Where they overlap, the slide turns white. This is the lecture's thesis made visual.
  • Semantic colours for cases: coral = the fail, mint = the win. Colour never carries meaning alone; every fail/win block also has a label and an icon. One round (14) has no fail or win — its two "Approach" cards share a single neutral violet, identical on both sides, precisely because neither is right or wrong.
  • Motion with a job: charts draw themselves when they appear, the two-circle "gap" visual closes as the lecture progresses, trick-question answers slide in on tap. No decorative motion. Respect reduced-motion settings.
  • Suggested stack for a hand-built feel: GSAP with ScrollTrigger for motion, Lenis for smooth scrolling, hand-drawn SVG charts from real data, Google Fonts (Bricolage Grotesque for display, Geist for text).

Structure

  • One idea per slide. Use progressive reveal when a slide has several points. Cognitive load.
  • Six chapters, each with a label and progress indicator. Feedback: people always know where they are.
  • Every versus round uses the same layout: principle on top, Fail on the left, Win on the right, interaction below. Mental models and consistency. Round 14 keeps the same layout with Approach A and Approach B instead, styled identically to each other, so the break in the pattern is visible, not just stated.
  • "In one line" recap strips after dense slides. Summaries after complex ideas.
  • Concrete example first, name of the principle second.

Screenshots and brands

  • Use your own screenshots of public interfaces where possible, cropped and annotated. For systems whose real screen is not public (Hawaii), use a clearly labelled recreation.
  • Use screenshots first, logos second. Brand marks should anchor a real case, not decorate a page. Keep them small, secondary, and paired with the product name.

Student version

  • The guidebook view keeps Main Content, the framework card, Resources and References, and hides speaker notes and build notes.

The gap

Purpose

State the central premise.

Main Content

Most bad design is not made by people trying to make something bad.

It happens because the people designing a system understand it far better than the people using it.

Every design is made for someone other than yourself.

Speaker Notes

That "someone" changes: a customer, a student, an employee, a government officer, a researcher, a developer using your API, or an AI model reading your prompt. Each arrives with different experience, expectations, culture, habits, motivation, attention and environment. A student on a crowded bus with 8% battery is a different user from the same student at a desk.

From practice: "Working with technology companies, I regularly see the engineering team, the marketing team and the customer describe the same product in three different ways. Nobody is wrong. They are standing in different places."

Visual Direction

The two particle clouds labelled What the designer knows (blue) and What the user understands (amber). The thin overlap is labelled What actually gets through.

You are not your user (1): the curse of knowledge

Purpose

Let students feel the curse of knowledge before naming it.

Main Content

Once you know something, it is very hard to imagine not knowing it.

This is the curse of knowledge.

In a Stanford study, people tapped the rhythm of famous songs. They predicted listeners would name the song half the time. Listeners got about 1 in 40.34

Speaker Notes

Run the game first. In pairs, one person taps the rhythm of a very well-known song (Indonesia Raya, Happy Birthday, a current hit) on the desk. Before tapping, they predict whether their partner will get it.

Ask tappers who thought it would be easy. Then ask listeners who got it.

The tapper hears the melody in their head. The listener hears knocking. That is what happens when a designer looks at their own interface: they "hear the melody" (what the icon means, where the setting lives, why the steps are in that order). The user hears knocking.

Visual Direction

Chart: two bars that draw on reveal. "Tappers predicted: 50%" and "Listeners got it: 2.5%".

Interaction

Predict Tapping game in pairs. Tappers predict their partner's success before tapping.

Reveal the answer

In the original study, tappers predicted 50%. Listeners named 3 of 120 songs (2.5%).

Speaker Notes

False consensus: in a classic study, students were asked whether they would walk around campus wearing a sign saying "Eat at Joe's". Whatever they chose, they estimated most other students would choose the same.5 Designers do this all the time: "I'd find this obvious, so users will too."

Mental models: if every shopping app a person has used puts the cart top-right, they will look there in yours. When a product breaks that expectation, users don't think "how innovative." They think "I can't find it."6

From practice: "In technology transfer, I sit between researchers and companies. A researcher describes a technology by what it can do in the lab. A company asks what problem it solves for their customer next quarter. Same technology, two mental models. If nobody translates, both leave the meeting thinking the other side didn't get it."

Trick question: did you read the terms?

Purpose

Show that people do not behave the way documents and interfaces assume they do.

Main Content

You sign up for a new social app. There's a Terms of Service box.

How long do people actually spend reading it?

A) Most read it properly B) About 5 minutes C) Under a minute

Speaker Notes

Let them vote, then reveal. Most will pick C and feel smart. Then deliver the second half: in the study, the fake terms included a clause saying users would give up their first-born child as payment. Almost nobody noticed.7

The design lesson is not "users are lazy." People make fast decisions about what deserves attention. If something important is buried in a wall of text, the design has already decided it won't be read. If you need people to understand something, the design has to carry it: short summaries, the key point at the moment it matters, plain language.

Visual Direction

Chart: "Expected reading time" versus "Actual reading time" for the privacy policy and the terms, as horizontal bars. The gap is the visual.

Interaction

Trick Question Vote A, B or C.

Reveal the answer

C. In a study with 543 participants signing up to a fake social network, people spent an average of 51 seconds on terms that needed about 15 to 17 minutes to read, and 73 seconds on a privacy policy that needed about 30 minutes. 98% missed the "gotcha" clauses, including one that asked for their first-born child.

What can they notice and remember?

Purpose

Explain cognitive load, recognition over recall and Hick's Law, then break a common assumption about reading.

Main Content

Cognitive load: people can hold only a few things in mind at once. Design move: ask only for what you need now. Remove, group or delay the rest.

Recognition over recall: recognising is easier than remembering. Design move: show recent searches, saved addresses, visible options.

Hick's Law: more choices, and harder choices, take longer to decide. Design move: highlight the few common choices. Put the rest one step away.

In one line: show less, show it at the right time, and don't make people remember.

Speaker Notes

Cognitive load: modern estimates suggest adults hold about four meaningful chunks in working memory, fewer than the famous "seven plus or minus two".910 The exact number matters less than the fact that the space is tiny.11

Recognition over recall: one of Nielsen's ten usability heuristics.1213

Hick's Law: measured with simple reaction-time tasks.14 Warn students against over-applying it. The famous "jam study" is often used to argue that fewer choices always sell more, but a meta-analysis of 50 experiments found the average choice-overload effect was close to zero.15 The lesson is to reduce the effort of choosing (grouping, good defaults, clear labels), not to remove choice.

Visual Direction

Interactive chart: a slider for "number of options" (2 to 40) and a curve showing how predicted decision time grows. Label it clearly as an illustrative model of Hick's Law, not measured data. Recap strip at the bottom.

Interaction

Trick Question "When you open a web page, how much of the text do you think people actually read?" Take guesses.

Reveal the answer

Jakob Nielsen's analysis of browsing data estimated people read at most about 28% of the words on an average page visit, and around 20% is more realistic.16 Design for scanning: headings, short sentences, the key point first.

What can they do easily?

Purpose

Explain Fitts's Law, feedback, and conventions, then let students predict user behaviour.

Main Content

Fitts's Law: big, close targets are faster and easier to hit than small, far ones. Design move: make key buttons large and easy to reach. Keep destructive buttons away from common ones.

Feedback: people need to know what just happened. Design move: respond to every action. Show progress for anything longer than about a second.

Conventions: people expect your product to work like the ones they already use. Design move: use familiar patterns unless you have a strong reason not to.

In one line: easy to reach, clear response, familiar patterns.

Speaker Notes

Fitts's Law comes from 1954 research on movement time.1718 On phones it becomes minimum touch-target sizes; WCAG 2.2 sets a minimum of 24 by 24 CSS pixels, and Apple recommends 44 by 44 points.19

Feedback: response-time research gives rules of thumb. About 0.1 seconds feels instant, about 1 second keeps the flow of thought, and around 10 seconds is where attention drifts.20 Google reported that 53% of mobile site visits are abandoned if a page takes longer than three seconds to load.21

Local example students know: ride-hailing and food-delivery apps show the driver moving on a map. The wait is the same length, but uncertainty drops. Research on "operational transparency" found that showing people the work being done can raise how much they value a service, even with longer waits.22 (That study tested travel and dating websites. Applying it to ride-hailing is a reasonable interpretation, not a direct finding.)

Conventions: Jakob's Law says users spend most of their time on other sites, so they expect yours to work the same way.23

Visual Direction

Start with the prediction question alone on screen. Then reveal the three principles. Visual aid: a timeline scale showing 0.1 s, 1 s, 3 s and 10 s, with the 3-second point marked "53% of mobile visits abandoned".

Interaction

Predict "You tap Pay on a checkout screen. Nothing changes for three seconds. What do you do?"

Reveal the answer

Most people tap again. Without feedback, a second tap can mean a double charge or a duplicate order. That is why good payment buttons change state immediately (spinner, then "Paid").

What makes them act?

Purpose

Explain friction, defaults and social proof, and set up the ethical edge the rounds will develop.

Main Content

Friction: small extra steps change whether people act at all. Design move: remove steps for things users want to do. Add steps only to protect them.

Defaults: most people keep whatever is pre-selected. Design move: the default is a decision you make for users. Choose it in their interest.

Social proof: people look at what others do when deciding what to do. Design move: honest information about others helps. Fake counts and fake scarcity destroy trust.

In one line: every design pushes behaviour somewhere. Decide on purpose, and in the user's interest.

Speaker Notes

Friction: families who got help completing the US student-aid form were more likely to submit it and enrol in college. The form was the barrier, not their motivation.24 BJ Fogg's model: behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet. Making things easier often beats making people more motivated.25

Defaults: automatic enrolment in a US workplace retirement plan raised participation among new employees dramatically.26 Organ-donor registration differs hugely between opt-in and opt-out countries.27

Social proof: UK tax letters saying most people in your area pay on time increased on-time payment in a large field trial.28

Honesty about effect sizes: nudges published in academic journals averaged an 8.7 percentage-point effect. The same kind of nudges run at scale by two US government nudge units averaged 1.4 points.29 Real, but much smaller. Test your own product; don't assume the textbook number.

Visual Direction

Same three-block layout as the previous principle sections. Chart: two bars, "Academic papers: 8.7 pp" and "At scale: 1.4 pp".

Interaction

Trick Question "Which button gets more clicks: red or green?"

Reveal the answer

In a widely shared test, a red button beat a green one by 21%.30 But the page was mostly green, so the red button simply stood out. The trick is that colour alone was never the answer. Contrast with the surroundings was. Anyone who says "red converts better" without context has learned the wrong lesson.

Chapter 3

From principles to practice

How practitioners turn this into rules

Purpose

Show how design teams translate psychology into working rules, and that different teams make different choices for different users.

Main Content

Five rules you'll see everywhere:

  1. Make important things obvious.
  2. Reduce unnecessary decisions.
  3. Show people what happened.
  4. Design around the user's goal, not your feature list.
  5. Use familiar conventions before inventing new ones.

Same rules, different users, different answers.

Speaker Notes

Where they come from: Steve Krug's Don't Make Me Think (1); GOV.UK's "Do the hard work to make it simple" (2); Nielsen's visibility of system status (3); GOV.UK's "Start with user needs" and IDEO's human-centred design (4); Jakob's Law and platform guidelines from Apple, Google and Microsoft (5).313233343536

These are not universal truths. GOV.UK designs for everyone in a country, including people with low confidence and old phones, so it favours plain, predictable pages. Apple's guidelines assume an Apple device and its gestures. Professional tools show many options at once because experts want speed over hand-holding.

Visual Direction

Five rules appear one at a time with a small source tag each. Then a three-way strip: "Public service", "Platform", "Pro tool", each with one line about its users.

Interaction

Burning Question "Should a banking app for your grandparents and a trading app for day traders follow the same rules?" Take one answer for each side.

This is not only about screens

Purpose

Widen the lens: anything made for other people is a design problem, including its name.

Main Content

Forms · dashboards · research posters · presentations · onboarding · internal tools · public services · AI prompts · even names.

Five questions that work for all of them:

  1. Who is using this?
  2. What are they trying to get done?
  3. What do they already understand?
  4. What might confuse them?
  5. What are they likely to do next?

Speaker Notes

This is the core of design thinking: understand the person and the problem before committing to a solution.37

Concrete examples: a group report your lecturer reads 40 of; a dashboard a manager checks in 30 seconds between meetings; an AI prompt where the "user" only knows what you wrote down (the curse of knowledge again).

Names are interfaces too. In 2024, Indonesian social media went viral over local-government apps with names like SIMONTOK (a food stock monitoring system) and SISEMOK (a community-organisation information system).38 The officials who chose them were thinking about acronyms. The public was thinking about something else entirely. One agency defended its name as a word from the local dialect, which is a perfect example of false consensus: it made sense to the people inside.

Lecturer note: some other names in that story are vulgar in Indonesian slang. Use the two above, and judge the room before going further.

Visual Direction

A cloud of the listed items, then the five questions large, one at a time. For the naming example, show each acronym large, then expand it letter by letter into its official meaning.

Interaction

Trick Question Show "SIMONTOK" alone. "What do you think this government app does?" Let the laughter happen.

Reveal the answer

Sistem Monitoring Stok dan Kebutuhan Pangan Pokok, a system for monitoring staple food stock. The designers read the acronym. Everyone else read the word.

Speaker Notes

Every handover loses meaning: intention becomes mockup, mockup becomes ticket, ticket becomes code. People who understand both sides cut the number of translations.

A developer who understands cognitive load and feedback makes hundreds of small decisions better: error messages, loading states, defaults. A designer who understands HTML, CSS and data flow designs things that can actually be built. Chapter 4

Good vs bad, same task every time

How the rounds work

Purpose

Set up the case section as a series of short, directly comparable rounds — and fix the one thing that makes case studies forgettable: a fail and a win that aren't actually doing the same job.

Main Content

Each round takes one task and shows it done badly, then well.

Not "a fail about friction" next to "a different company's win about something else that also involves friction." The same task. Cancelling a subscription. Launching a national system. Verifying who someone is.

Some pairs are the same product fixing its own mistake. Others are two products doing the identical job. Either way: if the fail and the win weren't solving the same problem, it isn't a fair comparison — say so out loud.

Thirteen of the fourteen rounds work exactly this way. One doesn't. Watch for which one, and don't assume it's a mistake sitting there waiting for you to name the fix — sometimes there isn't one.

Your job every round: name the principle before I do.

Speaker Notes

Explain the rule: when a case appears, students shout out or type which principle it breaks or uses. It keeps them thinking from the user's side instead of watching a list of stories.

Fourteen rounds is more than one session needs. Pick based on the room:

  • Cleanest set (the same product, before and after): Rounds 1 (Amazon), 7 (Hawaii) and 13 (Apple). Nobody can argue these are unfair comparisons — it's the same team fixing the same thing.
  • Strongest for an Indonesian audience: Rounds 2, 5, 6, 10 and 11.
  • Most serious ethical weight: Rounds 7, 9 and 10.
  • The one with no winner: Round 14. Everything before it trains the room to expect a fix. Use it to break that reflex on purpose — some differences are just two audiences, not a mistake and a correction.

Not every "win" is perfect, and several fails got fixed later, sometimes only after a fine or a government summons. That is part of the point: design gets corrected in public. Round 14 is there to stop that pattern from calcifying into "different equals wrong."

Visual Direction

A round index: fourteen tiles, each with the principle name and either a coral/mint fail-and-win pair or, for Round 14 only, a single neutral violet tile marked "no verdict." Tiles light up as each round is completed.

Interaction

Your Turn Ongoing game: first person to name the principle in each round gets the point.

Round 1 · Friction

Cancelling something

Purpose

Show the exact same task — cancelling a subscription — done to trap people, then done the way a regulator ordered it to be done instead.

Main Content

Principle: every extra step is a cost. The question is who pays it, and who decided.

The fail

Amazon Prime

Amazon Prime cancellation (US, 2023 to 2025)

The US Federal Trade Commission alleged that cancelling Prime took a four-page, six-click, fifteen-option flow, internally nicknamed "Iliad." Joining took one click. In September 2025 Amazon agreed to a $2.5 billion settlement, without admitting wrongdoing.4041

The fix

Amazon Prime

The same product, ordered to fix itself

The settlement requires Amazon to build "an easy way for consumers to cancel Prime, using the same method that consumers used to sign up."40 Cancelling now has to be exactly as hard as joining was — not easier than before, just no longer artificially harder.

This isn't the end of the story. In July 2025 a federal appeals court struck down the FTC's broader "click-to-cancel" rule, which would have required this of every subscription business, not just Amazon.42 In 2026 the FTC opened a new process to try again.43 The specific fix survives because it's written into Amazon's own settlement. The general rule for everyone else is still being fought over.

Speaker Notes

Same task, same product, forced correction. That's the cleanest kind of "fail vs win" there is: nobody can say "well, that's just a different app with different users." It's the same button.

Fogg's model helps here: people who wanted to cancel had the motivation. Amazon attacked their ability. The fix raises ability back to where sign-up already was.

Worth saying out loud: a court blocking the general rule doesn't undo Amazon's specific obligation. Regulation isn't one clean event — it's an ongoing argument, and the "win" here is narrower than it looks at first glance. Use this as a small model of reading a case study carefully instead of taking the headline at face value.

Other examples of the same principle, if you want more: Singapore's "Tell Us Once" (Myinfo) lets people fill government and bank forms with data the state already holds44. Korea's Toss says it cut a bank transfer to three steps at a time when banking meant browser plug-ins and certificates, and its user base grew 14-fold in about two years — a founder's own claim, not an independent audit, but backed by fast adoption.45

A single flow diagram, three rows: "Join Prime — 1 step" (mint), "Cancel Prime, before — 4 pages, 6 clicks, 15 options" (coral), "Cancel Prime, required now — same method as joining" (mint). The middle row is drawn much longer than the other two, to scale.

Interaction

Compare "Which app on your phone is easy to join and hard to leave? If you tried to cancel it right now, how many screens do you think it would take?" Have one or two students actually try, live, on their phone.

Round 2 · Context

Buying fuel without breaking the phone rule

Purpose

Show a company creating a physical-world contradiction, then quietly removing it — same task, same product, same country.

Main Content

Principle: users live in places, not in your office. A design that only works in a meeting room can still fail at the pump.

The fail

MyPertaminaPertamina

MyPertamina at the petrol pump (Indonesia, 2022)

From July 2022, in selected regions, buying subsidised Pertalite and Solar required registering and paying through the MyPertamina app. The internet asked the obvious question: if using a phone at a petrol station is discouraged for safety, why does the purchase itself require one? Pertamina had to clarify that phones were allowed only for payment, from inside the vehicle or 1.5 metres from the dispenser.46

The fix

Round 6 · Launch readiness

Purpose

Main Content

Principle: the first real users will always find what you missed. A launch strategy is really a decision about how many of them there are on day one.

The fail

Coretax

The new core tax system launched nationwide on 1 January 2025, to every taxpayer at once. Users reported failed logins, crashes, slow pages and having to re-enter data from the old systems. The tax directorate publicly apologised, the Finance Minister promised continued fixes, and the directorate waived administrative penalties for delays linked to the system.585960

The win

Britain is digitising the same kind of thing — how a country's taxpayers file — but in scheduled stages, by tax type and by income. VAT went first, phased in from April 2019 with all VAT-registered businesses in scope by April 2022. Income tax for the self-employed and landlords follows years later, and only above a threshold: over £50,000 in income from April 2026, over £30,000 from April 2027, over £20,000 from April 2028.61

Speaker Notes

Coretax had a good goal: replacing several separate tax applications with one system. The problem was scale and timing. A system that millions of taxpayers must use, with legal deadlines and penalties attached, met its first real users all at once. Every problem became everyone's problem, on the same day.

Making Tax Digital isn't finished and isn't free of its own controversy — the self-employed threshold has already slipped once. But the design decision it demonstrates is the direct opposite of Coretax's: start with the group best equipped to absorb problems (VAT-registered businesses, already used to digital bookkeeping), fix what breaks, then work down to smaller taxpayers over years, not weeks.

Keep the tone fair to Coretax. Large government systems are hard, and it did stabilise over the following months. The design lesson is about launch strategy — gradual rollout by segment, fallback options, feedback channels — decided before the deadline, not improvised after it.

Round 14 · Local convention

Whose "clean" is the standard?

Purpose

Break the pattern the last thirteen rounds trained into the room: that every fail has a fix. Show a real, well-documented design difference that isn't a mistake on either side, then make students argue a position instead of picking the obviously-correct answer — because this time there isn't one.

Main Content

Principle: what reads as clutter to one audience can read as evidence of value, effort and honesty to another. Visual restraint is not a universal marker of good design. It's one culture's convention, applied as if it were the default.

Approach A: Maximalist

TaobaoJD.comPinduoduoRakutenShopee

China and Japan's e-commerce homepages

Taobao, JD.com, Pinduoduo and Rakuten put an unusual amount on one screen at once: flash-sale countdown timers, price-slash badges, embedded livestreams, group-buy games and long blocks of text and reviews, all visible without scrolling.8889 Closer to home, Shopee runs the same playbook across Indonesia and the rest of Southeast Asia — in-app games like Shopee Tanam and spinning coin wheels are core screens, not side features.90

Approach B: Minimalist

AppleEverlaneAmazon

Western flagship e-commerce homepages

Apple's own store, Everlane and, in relative terms, Amazon favor large product photography, wide margins and a small number of choices per screen, pushing detail behind a click rather than showing all of it up front.

Speaker Notes

Don't let students reach for "one of these is just better design." Communication researcher Elizabeth Würtz coded real sites from high-context and low-context cultures side by side, building on Edward T. Hall's original distinction between the two.91 High-context sites, which much of East Asia fits, used what she called a "montage, layer-upon-layer" visual style: many colours, fonts and competing shapes at once. Low-context sites, which much of Scandinavia and the Anglophone West fit, used tabular, functional layouts with information tiled cleanly side by side.88 That's a description of two different communication styles, not a verdict on which one is competent.

Hall's underlying distinction: in a high-context culture, the receiver is expected to do more interpretive work, and value is signalled through context rather than spelled out. In a low-context culture, the sender is expected to make everything explicit and legible on its own. A dense homepage isn't failing to communicate clearly. In its own register, "everything visible at once" reads as complete and trustworthy, and a mostly-empty screen can read as unfinished, or like something is being hidden.

Give students the honest complication, because it's real. Nielsen Norman Group user-tested this directly: Chinese participants described dense layouts as "normal" and "traditional" and complained less about them than about simplified versions, but they still took measurably longer to find things on the complex sites, and some hit real friction ("Where is the share via email button? I don't know… there are too many options.").89 The researchers' own conclusion pushes back on pure relativism: density is tolerated and even preferred up to a point, but that doesn't mean every last badge and banner is earning its place. Cultural fit explains the baseline. It doesn't excuse every individual decision on top of it.

Give them the exception that keeps this from hardening into a stereotype, too: MUJI is a Japanese brand, and its site is one of the most minimalist in retail, because "no-brand, quality-first" is MUJI's actual brand position — and that position reads clearly precisely because it breaks the category convention on purpose. National culture sets the default. A specific brand can still spend restraint (or density) as a deliberate signal against that backdrop.

Tie it back to the earlier bias sections: this is the curse of knowledge and false consensus operating at the level of an entire industry's design culture, not just one person's. A designer trained in one convention who assumes "clean" is objectively better isn't wrong about their own market. They're making the same mistake as the designers behind every fail in this chapter, one level up: treating their own default as everyone's default.

None is "here's an unrelated success story to make you feel better" — that was the whole complaint worth fixing about this section, and it's worth naming to students directly: a fair comparison holds the task constant and changes only the choices.

None of these teams were stupid, and most were very skilled. Every fail came from people looking at their product from their own position: the launch date, the company metric, the acronym that looked clever, the design trend they loved. Every fix or win came from someone going back to the exact same job and doing it with the user standing in the room.

Then flag Round 14 by name: it's in this table to stop the pattern from becoming a reflex. Thirteen rounds train the room to expect a villain and a hero every time. If that reflex goes unchecked, it turns into exactly the kind of thinking this whole lecture argues against — assuming any difference from your own default is automatically a defect. Round 14 has no villain. Make sure the room notices that before moving to Chapter 5.

Learning to do this

How do you get good at this?

Purpose

Move from "what good design looks like" to "how you build the skill".

Main Content

Good designers have seen a lot of design, and questioned it.

Every app you use is free practice:

  1. Why was this designed this way?
  2. What else could the designer have chosen?
  3. Who would the alternative be better for?

Look closely at: onboarding · error messages · checkout · empty screens · settings · cancellation

Then: watch real people · interview users · prototype · test · revise

Speaker Notes

Compare two apps doing the same job: two e-wallets, two food-delivery apps, two banking apps. Screenshot five onboarding flows and lay them side by side.

The third question is the one people skip. Every design choice is better for some people and worse for others. Finding who it fails is where the insight is.

Watch people. Sit next to a parent, a younger sibling or a friend from another faculty while they use something, and don't help. Ten minutes of that teaches more than any article.92

What AI changes, and what it doesn't

Purpose

Give a realistic view of AI in design work.

Main Content

AI can help you: generate interface variations · draft interview questions · summarise research · build quick prototypes · write code · brainstorm edge cases

You still need to: watch real people · catch wrong assumptions · judge AI suggestions · understand culture and context · make tradeoffs

AI can speed up iteration. It does not remove the need to understand people.

Speaker Notes

Use AI where being wrong is cheap: variations, first drafts, code scaffolding.

Be careful where being wrong is expensive: understanding real users. Nielsen Norman Group tested AI-generated "synthetic users" against studies with real people. Their conclusion was that user research needs real users; the synthetic answers were shallow and too positive.9394

AI training data under-represents many contexts, including many Indonesian users, languages and devices. Local knowledge is a real advantage.

Purpose

A checklist of traps, each now linked to a case students have seen.

Main Content

  1. Designing for yourself. (SIMONTOK)
  2. Asking everyone. More feedback is not better feedback. Ask the people who will use it.
  3. Listening to noise. Opinions without context aren't evidence. Watch behaviour.
  4. Launching to everyone at once. (Coretax)

Purpose

Main Content

THE SOMEONE-ELSE CHECK

How will someone else understand this?

BEFORE you build WHILE you build AFTER you build

Close: reduce the gap

Purpose

End on the central idea.

Main Content

Good design is not about making interfaces look impressive.

It's about reducing the distance between what the designer intends and what the user understands.

Psychology shows you the gap. Technology lets you build across it. Research tells you if you were right. Iteration closes it a little more each time.

Next time you build anything, ask: how will someone else understand this?

Speaker Notes

Don Norman described two gulfs: the gulf of execution (can I work out how to do what I want?) and the gulf of evaluation (can I tell what happened?). Every principle and every round today was about closing one of the two.97

Burning questions for Q&A

Purpose

Make the Q&A a debate, not a silence.

Main Content

Pick one. Argue it from the user's side.

  1. Is making cancellation hard just good business?
  2. Should apps be allowed to design for addiction if users say they enjoy it?
  3. If an AI designs the interface, who is responsible when users get hurt?
  4. Should government apps be tested with citizens before launch, by law?

If the room is quiet, pick question 1 and split the room in half by seating. Give each side 60 seconds to prepare.

Useful follow-ups:

  • On ethics: pushing users is more defensible when it serves the user's own goal, is transparent, and is easy to reverse. Test each round against those three.
